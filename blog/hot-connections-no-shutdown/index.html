How Thermography Finds Hot Connections Without a Shutdown

By Utilisight Corp·Nov 07, 2026·2 min read

Loose or corroded connections on overhead distribution lines can lead to outages and equipment damage. Finding them used to mean close-up inspection or planned outages. Thermal imaging can flag suspect connections from a distance while lines stay energized and in service.

Why bad connections heat up

A connection heats up when resistance rises at a current-carrying joint. Joule's law describes it:

P = I² R

  • P = heat generated (watts)
  • I = current (amperes)
  • R = resistance of the connection (ohms)

A healthy connector or splice has very low resistance, well under a milliohm. Over time, connections degrade:

  • Wind vibration loosens hardware.
  • Heating and cooling cycles cause expansion and contraction.
  • Oxidation and contamination build up resistance on contact surfaces.

As resistance rises, the joint produces more heat and runs warmer than similar connections nearby.

Why from the air

Radiometric thermal cameras detect long-wave infrared energy (roughly 8–14 µm) and estimate a temperature for every pixel.

  • A better view: cutout tops, bushing connections and jumpers can be hidden from the ground by crossarms and insulators. A drone can view them from above and from the side.
  • Comparison, not absolute temperature: bare metal reflects heat and gives unreliable absolute readings. Comparing a connection with the same component on the other phases, under the same load, is more reliable.
  • No interruption: thermal cameras only receive the heat that hardware gives off, so lines stay energized and customers stay connected.

Finding hot connections under load helps move maintenance from emergency repairs to planned, condition-based work.
