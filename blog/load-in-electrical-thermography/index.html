Why Load Matters in Electrical Thermography

By Utilisight Corp·Nov 07, 2026·X min read

A thermal reading only means something when you know how much current the circuit was carrying. On a lightly loaded line, a degraded connection may barely stand out, then overheat at peak demand.

Heat rises with the square of current

Heat at a connection is proportional to the square of the current. Twice the current produces four times the heat; four times the current produces sixteen times the heat.

Example: a degraded splice with a resistance of 1 milliohm (0.001 Ω).

Load Current Heat at the splice
Off-peak 50 A 50² × 0.001 = 2.5 W
Peak 200 A 200² × 0.001 = 40 W

Four times the current produces sixteen times the heat. A connection that looks minor off-peak can become significant at peak.

Adjusting for load

Thermographers often estimate how a reading would look at a higher load. Example: a connection 4 °C warmer than its neighbours, measured at 30% load, could be about 44 °C warmer at full load (4 × (1 ÷ 0.3)² ≈ 44 °C).

Real heating doesn't follow this formula exactly, because wind, sun and the component's own cooling also matter. Treat adjusted values as estimates for prioritizing follow-up, not as measurements. Readings taken at very low load are the least reliable to adjust.

When to inspect

  • Load: inspections are commonly recommended at 40% of rated load or more. Below that, problems can be missed.
  • Peak season: in Ontario, distribution load is often highest in summer, when air conditioning runs, especially in the late afternoon and evening.
  • Load data: asking the utility or owner for load at the time of the flight lets each reading be judged in context.
  • Calm, dry conditions: wind cools connections and can make a problem look smaller; direct sun can heat components and create false readings.
