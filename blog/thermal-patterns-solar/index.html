5 Thermal Patterns Every Solar Owner Should Know

By Utilisight Corp·Nov 07, 2026·3 min read

Thermal imaging gives a fast, non-contact view of how a solar array is performing. Panels that heat unevenly often point to electrical or physical issues that can reduce production. Below are five common patterns and what each may indicate. Each one is a reason to investigate, not a diagnosis: confirming the cause takes electrical testing on site.

1. Single hot cell

What it looks like: one bright, warmer cell within a panel.

What it may indicate: cell cracking (from transport, installation or hail), soiling, debris, localized shading or a manufacturing defect.

Why it matters: a damaged or shaded cell can stop producing and heat up as current from neighbouring cells flows through it. Over time, severe hot spots can damage the backsheet and other panel materials.

2. Bypass diode pattern

What it looks like: a uniformly warmer strip covering about one-third of a panel, sometimes two-thirds.

What it may indicate: an active or failed bypass diode. Most panels have three diodes, and each one switches a section out of the circuit when cells in it are shaded or damaged.

Why it matters: while a diode is active, that section (about one-third of the panel's output) isn't producing. A diode that stays active with no shading present needs follow-up.

3. Panel or string not producing

What it looks like: a whole panel, or a full row of panels, slightly and evenly warmer than its neighbours.

What it may indicate: an open circuit, such as a disconnected connector, a blown fuse, a tripped breaker or an inverter input fault.

Why it matters: when no current flows, panels convert none of the sunlight to electricity and run a few degrees warmer. An offline string loses all of its output until the fault is fixed.

4. Patchwork pattern (possible PID)

What it looks like: a patchwork of warmer cells, often near the panel frame, and most noticeable in panels near one end of a string.

What it may indicate: a pattern consistent with potential-induced degradation (PID), where leakage current between the cells and the grounded frame reduces performance. Heat, humidity and high system voltage make it more likely.

Why it matters: PID can cause significant power loss across affected strings. Caught early, it can often be partly reversed with corrective equipment. Electrical testing confirms it.

5. Hot connector or junction box

What it looks like: a warm spot at a connector, or at the junction box location on a panel, sometimes spreading to nearby cells.

What it may indicate: a loose, corroded or poorly crimped connection, or degraded wiring inside the junction box.

Why it matters: resistance at a bad connection turns current into heat (P = I² × R). Left alone, an overheating connection can damage components and become a fire risk.

For reliable results, solar inspections are flown on clear days with strong sunlight on the panels (at least 600 W/m², following IEC TS 62446-3 guidance) and the system producing. In Ontario, that usually means May to September.
